Ocean Sound Monitoring

The Channel Islands National Marine Sanctuary (CINMS) surrounds the five northern Channel Islands and is situated across the Santa Barbara Channel. Within the Channel, two powerful currents, the northbound Davidson Current and the southbound California Current, meet and respective warm and cool waters mix. That mixing combined with seasonal high winds make this region extremely productive. This productivity makes CINMS a global foraging hotspot for marine life, like whales and fish. The CINMS region consistently has some of the highest reports of commercial fishing value and volume. At the same time, this region is also home to consistent commercial ship traffic transiting through established Traffic Separation Schemes, acting as major highways for large vessels. Much of the region is also inside a Naval Testing and Training area, and Vandenberg SpaceForce Base has signifantly increased launch activites that go over CINMS since 2024.

The bustling activity of both biological and human activity in this region has been monitored using passive acoustics for decades. A Noise Reference Station has been in place on the backside of Santa Cruz Island since 2014. Scripps Instituion of Oceanography has been monitoring underwater sound across the Southern California Bight at many locations for many years, including at Site B inside the Santa Barbara Channel—a listening station that provides Vessel Speed Reduction noise measureuments to the shipping industry each year during voluntary slowing periods. CINMS began monitoring sanctuary soundscapes in 2018, with the establishment of CI-01 and CI-04 located to the north and south of Santa Rosa Island, respectively. Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution maintains a near real-time buoy that provides large baleen whale detections at whalesafe.com.

To see historical CINMS monitoring sites that are no longer active, please visit Sanctuary Soundscape Project data portal.

Ocean Sound Conditions

Soundscapes vary across years, seasons, and even within a day. Differences are driven by shifts in wind and weather patterns, migration and behavior of animals, and patterns in human activities. We track how sound levels change in different frequencies using standardized soundscape metrics to understand these changes. Seasonal and annual percentiles of all the data are used to define typical conditions.

What are the seasonal patterns across frequencies?

Season is often a main driver of soundscape differences: wind and weather patterns shift, species migrate or change behavior, and humans change their marine activities. As shown on the graphic(s) below, we can visualize the differences by looking at the variation across frequency (pitch of the sound in Hertz) shown on the x-axis and intensity (how loud the sound is in decibels) shown on the y-axis. The colored lines represent seasonal categories, vertical lines are the start of a frequency range that continues over the shaded area that a sound source of interest can be heard, and the black lines bound the soundscape to expected range of intensity based on when only wind noise is present.
Here are some questions to consider when viewing the graph(s) below:
(1) Which season has the highest sound levels?
(2) Are there peaks in the sound levels for any of the sounds of interest?
(3) Are the low-frequency sound levels outside the expected range for wind noise?

Processing audio to calibrated sound levels (aka soundscape metrics) involves multiple steps to get a specified time frequency data product. All comparisons in the ONMS soundscape inventory reports are hourly power spectral densities in 1 Hz for each third-octave band center frequency. Data processed during the Sanctuary Soundscape Project (pre- 2022 ONMS Sound data) used the Triton Soundscape Remora and data processed for the ONMS sound used either MANTA or PyPAM . Both processing softwares calculated power spectral density (PSD) levels as the median of mean-square pressure amplitude (μPa2). Triton outputs a resolution of 1 Hz/1-sec. MANTA and PyPAM outputs a resolution of hybrid milli-decidade per second. Triton uses 0% overlap in spectral decompositions and MANTA/PyPAM uses 50% overlap, resulting in slight differences in values between these processing softwares. Triton then calculated PSD levels per hour as the median of mean-square pressure amplitude (μPa2) with a resolution of 1 Hz from 20 Hz to 24,000 Hz over no less than 1800 seconds in each hour and converted to decibels (dB re 1 μPa2/Hz). Third-octave band sound pressure levels (TOLs) then were calculated by integration of PSD levels over each octave band with nominal center frequencies. MANTA and PyPAM do the same calculation as Triton to get PSD levels from 1 Hz/1 sec PSDs to hourly but at a 1-minute resolution and within the hybrid millie-decide bands. This difference in time averaging impacts the comparability of the results, and caution should be used when reviewing results that include SanctSound Triton generated hourly data (e.g. TOL_1h.csv). Compatibility between PyPAM and Manata were verified.
For the MANTA and PyPAM results PAMscapes was used to calculate one-third octave bands (using createOctaveLevel(hmdData, type='tol') and then the median for each hour with binSoundscapeData(cDataTOL, bin = "1hour", method = c("median")). Hourly data were then matched with an estimate of wind speed at that location using matchGFS in PAMscapes (Global Forecast System (GFS) weather model). Hourly TOLs were then converted back to 1-Hz resolution to match the wind model results (dB re 1 μPa/Hz).

How are ocean sound conditions changing across years?

Comparing annual ocean sound levels over time can track changes in ocean noise and its contributors. Efforts to reduce noise impacts to marine animals are underway on local to global scales. Strategies can include avoidance of times and areas with high vulnerability, changing the operation of a noise source (e.g., by slowing down) or through the design and use of alternative, quieter sources (vessel speed reduction strategies). The effectiveness of these approaches, and the scales over which they are effective, can be tracked through comprehensive monitoring efforts. A focused analysis is often necessary to tease apart the multiple drivers of ocean sound levels, however, annual summaries provide initial insights to overall patterns.

(1) Are levels lower in the most recent year of monitoring in any of the frequencies of interest?

Is the intensity (loudness) of sound sources of interest within typical range?

In some soundscapes, we can use specific frequencies as indicators for the presence of a source of interest (e.g. species presence and behavior). Monitoring sites are often chosen because a known source is present and we want to track that source. These sources need to be the dominant sound energy contributor to the specific frequency or frequency range. For example, seasonal migration of humpback whales to Hawaiian Islands results in soundscapes dominated by their calling between 100 and 1,000 Hz. At sites near commercial shipping lanes, 63 and 125 Hz TOL are used as an indicator for ship noise. At sites where snapping shrimp are present, 4,000 - 10,000 Hz can be used as an indicator of their sounds. Ocean Sound Indicators

We can use long-term monitoring of ocean sound to derive and track indicators of ocean conditions. These indicators track the status and trend of habitat condition, species presence, human-use patterns, and management activities. When are the seasonal biologically-important times for whales?

The Office of National Marine Sanctuaries (ONMS) maintains two CINMS listening stations with the Naval Postgraduate School, CI01 and CI04. We partnered with Moss Landing Marine Laboratories and Stanford Hopkins Marine Station to analyze data from this site for humpback whale vocalization, both song and non-song calls, to understand seasonality and potential behavior associated with calls over time. Visit Acoustic Presence of Humpback Whales in U.S. West Coast National Marine Sanctuaries and Unraveling Mysteries of Humpback Whale Song for more information.
