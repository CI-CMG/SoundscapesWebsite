Stellwagen Bank National Marine Sanctuary Soundscape Report

Published

July 8, 2025

Where are we listening?

Ocean sound monitoring within Stellwagen Bank National Marine Sanctuary (SBNMS) has a long history with a focus on monitoring marine mammals, fishes and vessel noise to inform the status and trends of human activities and marine mammals. Multiple sites in the sanctuary were part of the Sanctuary Soundscape Monitoring Project, a 5-year (2018 to 2022) system-wide monitoring project to understand the diversity of sounds in sanctuary waters.

Ocean sound monitoring and analysis is maintained at three sites within SBNMS. Two sites (SB01, SB03) capture vessel activity and sounds in higher use areas of the sanctuary. A third site (NRS09) captures migrating whale patterns and vessel traffic in an area closed to some activities, and is part of the U.S. Ocean Noise Reference Station Network.

Current ONMS ocean sound monitoring and analysis is maintained at three sites. SB01 is a historical monitoring site that supports seasonally high abundances of Atlantic cod and is located near a region of elevated commercial vessel activity. SB03 is a shallow sandy habitat situated on the bank that supports the foraging and social activities of marine mammals. And, the Noise Reference Station site (NRS09) has a long term low frequency recording with more propagation exposure to longer distance sounds and in a place protected from fishing, known as a Dedicated Habitat Research Area.

To see historical monitoring sites that are no longer active, please visit Sanctuary Soundscape Project data portal

Ocean sound conditions

Soundscapes vary across years, seasons, and even within a day. Differences are driven by shifts in wind and weather patterns, migration and behavior of animals, and patterns in human activities. We track how sound levels change in different frequencies using standardized soundscape metrics to understand these changes. Seasonal and annual percentiles of all the data are used to define typical conditions.

Seasonal conditions

Season is often a main driver of soundscape differences: wind and weather patterns shift, species migrate or change behavior, and humans change their marine activities. As shown on the graphic(s) below, we can visualize the differences by looking at the variation across frequency (pitch of the sound in Hertz) shown on the x-axis and intensity (how loud the sound is in decibels) shown on the y-axis. The colored lines represent seasonal categories, vertical lines are the start of a frequency range that continues over the shaded area that a sound source of interest can be heard, and the black lines bound the soundscape to expected range of intensity based on when only wind noiseis present.
Here are some questions to consider when viewing the graph(s) below:
(1) Which season has the highest sound levels?
(2) Are there peaks in the sound levels for any of the sounds of interest?
(3) Are the low-frequency sound levels outside the expected range for wind noise?

The median power spectral densities (PSD) for all hours across all years are calculated from calibrated audio data using community software tools: Soundscape Metrics, MANTA, or PyPAM. Triton software calculates the one-third octave band sound pressure levels by integration of PSD levels with a 1 Hz/1 second resolution and a median was used to calculate hourly values over no less than 1,800 1-s values for that hour and converted to decibels (dB re 1 μPa). MANTA and PYPAM software calculates power spectral density (PSD) levels per minute (μPa²) within the hybrid milledecade frequency bands. PAMscapes was used to calculate the median for each hour within one-third octave bands. These values were then converted to 1-Hz resolution to match the wind model results by converting to pressure and dividing by the band width before converting back to sound spectrum levels in decibels (dB re 1 μPa/Hz).

How do soundscape conditions compare across sites? We can use other monitoring sites in similar oceanographic settings (e.g. coastal or offshore) to visualize differences. Data collected across sites during a dedicated monitoring period, like the Sanctuary SoundScape Monitoring Project, can provide initial insights on the differences. In this visualization, soundscape conditions across multiple sites are shown in a specific month, and the black curve represents the site in this sanctuary. What is different? What is the same? Annual conditions

Comparing annual ocean sound levels over time can track changes in ocean noise and its contributors. Efforts to reduce noise impacts to marine animals are underway on local to global scales. Strategies can include avoidance of times and areas with high vulnerability, changing the operation of a noise source (e.g., by slowing down) or through the design and use of alternative, quieter sources (vessel speed reduction strategies. The effectiveness of these approaches, and the scales over which they are effective, can be tracked through comprehensive monitoring efforts. A focused analysis is often necessary to tease apart the multiple drivers of ocean sound levels, however, annual summaries provide initial insights to overall patterns.

(1) Are levels lower in the most recent year of monitoring in any of the frequencies of interest?

How often are sources of interest likely present?

When sound levels deviate from expected based on wind speed and depth of the monitoring site, other sources are likely present in the soundscape. While this metric does not distinguish between types of sources (e.g. vocalizing species or human activity), it does indicate what percent of the time the soundscape contains sounds of interest to resource managers. When shifts in this metric are observed, further analysis is recommended to understand what is driving the shifts in patterns. For example, are species shifting the timing of their presence in the area?

Are sounds of interest within typical conditions?

In some soundscapes, we can use specific frequencies as indicators for the presence of a source of interest (e.g. species presence and behavior). Monitoring sites are often chosen because a known source is present and we want to track that source. These sources need to be the dominant sound energy contributor to the specific frequency or frequency range. For example, seasonal migration of humpback whales to Hawaiian Islands results in soundscapes dominated by their calling. At sites near commercial shipping lanes, 63 Hz and 125 Hz are used as an indicator for ship noise. We can use long-term monitoring of ocean sound to derive and track indicators of ocean conditions. These indicators track the status and trend of habitat condition, species presence, human-use patterns, and management activities. How much noise from large vessels may be interfering with whale communication?

Sound levels rise when ships are present in an area due to engine and propeller noise. We used a monthly metric to assess the level of noise interference with biological communication. To calculate the noise added when a ship was nearby (transiting within 10 km), ocean sound data was combined with ship tracking data. Nearby ship presence is tracked using data from the Automatic Identification System (AIS), an onboard transponder that most ships are required to use to transmit their location and identity. Higher median values indicate more interference with biological communication at the specified frequency.

How does the level of traffic impact change conditions?

Ship traffic can vary and this may influence the level of noise in the environment. Periods with different numbers of ships transiting nearby were compared to understand the difference: no ships, low number of ships ( less than 3), medium number of ships (3 to 5 ships ), and high number of ships (more than 5).

When do right whales contribute to the soundscape, indicating their presence nearby?

The National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) maintains the analysis of presence of North Atlantic Right whales from passive acoustic monitoring data within U.S. waters, including ONMS sites. Visit the interactive website to see spatial and temporal patterns in species presence.
