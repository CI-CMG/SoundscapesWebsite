Ocean Sound Conditions

Soundscapes vary across years, seasons, and even within a day. Differences are driven by shifts in wind and weather patterns, migration and behavior of animals, and patterns in human activities. We track how sound levels change in different frequencies using standardized soundscape metrics to understand these changes. Seasonal and annual percentiles of all the data are used to define typical conditions.

What are the seasonal patterns across frequencies?

Season is often a main driver of soundscape differences: wind and weather patterns shift, species migrate or change behavior, and humans change their marine activities. As shown on the graphic(s) below, we can visualize the differences by looking at the variation across frequency (pitch of the sound in Hertz) shown on the x-axis and intensity (how loud the sound is in decibels) shown on the y-axis. The colored lines represent seasonal categories, vertical lines are the start of a frequency range that continues over the shaded area that a sound source of interest can be heard, and the black lines bound the soundscape to expected range of intensity based on when only wind noiseis present.
Here are some questions to consider when viewing the graph(s) below:
(1) Which season has the highest sound levels?
(2) Are there peaks in the sound levels for any of the sounds of interest?
(3) Are the low-frequency sound levels outside the expected range for wind noise?

How are ocean sound conditions changing across years?

Comparing annual ocean sound levels over time can track changes in ocean noise and its contributors. Efforts to reduce noise impacts to marine animals are underway on local to global scales. Strategies can include avoidance of times and areas with high vulnerability, changing the operation of a noise source (e.g., by slowing down) or through the design and use of alternative, quieter sources (vessel speed reduction strategies. The effectiveness of these approaches, and the scales over which they are effective, can be tracked through comprehensive monitoring efforts. A focused analysis is often necessary to tease apart the multiple drivers of ocean sound levels, however, annual summaries provide initial insights to overall patterns.
Here are some questions to consider when viewing the graph(s) below:
(1) Are levels lower in the most recent year of monitoring in any of the frequencies of interest?
(2) Are there peaks in the sound levels for any of the sounds of interest? Do they differ across years?

How often are sources of interest likely present?

When sound levels deviate from expected based on wind speed and depth of the monitoring site, other sources are likely present in the soundscape. While this metric does not distinguish between types of sources (e.g. vocalizing species or human activity), it does indicate what percent of the time the soundscape contains sounds of interest to resource managers. When shifts in this metric are observed, further analysis is recommended to understand what is driving the shifts in patterns. For example, are species shifting the timing of their presence in the area? Note that any missing bars in the graphic below indicate no sound recording effort for that month.

Are sounds of interest within typical range?

In some soundscapes, we can use specific frequencies as indicators for the presence of a source of interest (e.g. species presence and behavior). Monitoring sites are often chosen because a known source is present and we want to track that source. These sources need to be the dominant sound energy contributor to the specific frequency or frequency range. For example, seasonal migration of humpback whales to Hawaiian Islands results in soundscapes dominated by their calling between 200 and 1,000 Hz. At sites near commercial shipping lanes, 63 Hz and 125 Hz are used as an indicator for ship noise. At sites where snapping shrimp are present, 10,000 Hz is used as an indicator of their sounds. Ocean Sound Indicators

We can use long-term monitoring of ocean sound to derive and track indicators of ocean conditions. These indicators track the status and trend of habitat condition, species presence, human-use patterns, and management activities. When do fish choruses contribute to the soundscape?

Knowning when fish are calling helps identify important biological periods for fish species. A reduction in noise during these periods benefits fish communication. When do whales contribute to the soundscape, indicating their presence nearby?

The National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) maintains the analysis of acoustic presence of many whale species from passive acoustic monitoring data within U.S. waters, including ONMS sites. Visit the interactive website to explore spatial and temporal patterns in different species.
