Cordell Bank National Marine Sanctuary Soundscape Report

Published

January 29, 2026

Ocean sound monitoring

Cordell Bank National Marine Sanctuary (CBNMS) sits offshore of California, near San Francisco. CBNMS is bordered by Greater Farallones National Marine Sanctuary (CGNMF) which extends north, south, and towards the coastline. Ocean sound monitoring began in fall 2015 to record low-frequency, ambient sounds in the sanctuary waters. The monitoring location is part of a national soundscape monitoring program, the Noise Reference Station (NRS) Network, which is a collaborative effort with ONMS, the National Marine Fisheries Service, the National Park Service, and the Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory. The NRS site in CBNMS is deployed near the southern border of the sanctuary abutting GFNMS. The instrument is suspended in deep water (~550m) to maximize the listening range of the hydrophone for low-frequency sounds generated within sanctuary waters. Baseline soundscape conditions from the first years of sampling (2015-2017) described seasonal baleen whale presence and ongoing vessel noise propagating from the shipping lanes leading to San Francisco and Oakland.

Monitoring continues at this location to capture sound associated with vessel traffic coming in/out of the Ports of San Francisco and Oakland to the south and west. It's also an area with high baleen whale occurrence seasonally.

Ocean Sound Conditions

Soundscapes vary across years, seasons, and even within a day. Differences are driven by shifts in wind and weather patterns, migration and behavior of animals, and patterns in human activities. We track how sound levels change in different frequencies using standardized soundscape metrics to understand these changes. Seasonal and annual percentiles of all the data are used to define typical conditions.

What are the seasonal patterns across frequencies?

Season is often a main driver of soundscape differences: wind and weather patterns shift, species migrate or change behavior, and humans change their marine activities. As shown on the graphic(s) below, we can visualize the differences by looking at the variation across frequency (pitch of the sound in Hertz) shown on the x-axis and intensity (how loud the sound is in decibels) shown on the y-axis. The colored lines represent seasonal categories, vertical lines are the start of a frequency range that continues over the shaded area that a sound source of interest can be heard, and the black lines bound the soundscape to expected range of intensity based on when only wind noise is present.

(1) Which season has the highest sound levels?
(2) Are there peaks in the sound levels for any of the sounds of interest?

Processing audio to calibrated sound levels (aka soundscape metrics) involves multiple steps to get a specified time frequency data product. All comparisons in the ONMS soundscape inventory reports are hourly power spectral densities in 1 Hz for each third-octave band center frequency. Data processed during the Sanctuary Soundscape Project (pre- 2022 ONMS Sound data) used the Triton Soundscape Remora and data processed for the ONMS sound used either MANTA or PyPAM . Both processing softwares calculated power spectral density (PSD) levels as the median of mean-square pressure amplitude (μPa2). Triton outputs a resolution of 1 Hz/1-sec. MANTA and PyPAM outputs a resolution of hybrid milli-decidade per second. Triton uses 0% overlap in spectral decompositions and MANTA/PyPAM uses 50% overlap, resulting in slight differences in values between these processing softwares. Triton then calculated PSD levels per hour as the median of mean-square pressure amplitude (μPa2) with a resolution of 1 Hz from 20 Hz to 24,000 Hz over no less than 1800 seconds in each hour and converted to decibels (dB re 1 μPa2/Hz). Third-octave band sound pressure levels (TOLs) then were calculated by integration of PSD levels over each octave band with nominal center frequencies. MANTA and PyPAM do the same calculation as Triton to get PSD levels from 1 Hz/1 sec PSDs to hourly but at a 1-minute resolution and within the hybrid millie-decide bands. This difference in time averaging impacts the comparability of the results, and caution should be used when reviewing results that include SanctSound Triton generated hourly data (e.g. TOL_1h.csv). Compatibility between PyPAM and Manata were verified.
For the MANTA and PyPAM results PAMscapes was used to calculate one-third octave bands (using createOctaveLevel(hmdData, type='tol') and then the median for each hour with binSoundscapeData(cDataTOL, bin = "1hour", method = c("median")). Hourly data were then matched with an estimate of wind speed at that location using matchGFS in PAMscapes (Global Forecast System (GFS) weather model). Hourly TOLs were then converted back to 1-Hz resolution to match the wind model results (dB re 1 μPa/Hz).

How are ocean sound conditions changing across years?

Comparing annual ocean sound levels over time can track changes in ocean noise and its contributors. Efforts to reduce noise impacts to marine animals are underway on local to global scales. Strategies can include avoidance of times and areas with high vulnerability, changing the operation of a noise source (e.g., by slowing down) or through the design and use of alternative, quieter sources (vessel speed reduction strategies). The effectiveness of these approaches, and the scales over which they are effective, can be tracked through comprehensive monitoring efforts. A focused analysis is often necessary to tease apart the multiple drivers of ocean sound levels, however, annual summaries provide initial insights to overall patterns.

How often are sources of interest likely present?

When sound levels deviate from expected based on wind speed and depth of the monitoring site, other sources are likely present in the soundscape. While this metric does not distinguish between types of sources (e.g. vocalizing species or human activity), it does indicate what percent of the time the soundscape contains sounds of interest to resource managers. When shifts in this metric are observed, further analysis is recommended to understand what is driving the shifts in patterns. For example, are species shifting the timing of their presence in the area? Note that any missing bars in the graphic below indicate no sound recording effort for that month.

Are sounds of interest within typical range?

In some soundscapes, we can use specific frequencies as indicators for the presence of a source of interest (e.g. species presence and behavior). Monitoring sites are often chosen because a known source is present and we want to track that source. These sources need to be the dominant sound energy contributor to the specific frequency or frequency range. For example, seasonal migration of humpback whales to Hawaiian Islands results in soundscapes dominated by their calling between 200 and 1,000 Hz. At sites near commercial shipping lanes, 63 Hz and 125 Hz are used as an indicator for ship noise. At sites where snapping shrimp are present, 10,000 Hz is used as an indicator of their sounds. What are the trends in ocean noise?

The purpose of the Ocean Noise Reference Station network is to track trends in low-frequency ocean noise. Year-1 of monitoring was used as a reference point. The monthly median sound levels during the reference year were calculated for the low-frequency sound environment, specifically 63 and 125 Hz. Changes from the reference year are presented as (1) the difference in decibels from reference monthly median values (positive means louder, negative means quieter), and (2) time above the reference monthly median by comparing the hourly values to the reference value and tallying for how much of the time is above.

How much noise from large vessels may be interfering with whale communication?

Sound levels rise when ships are transiting in an area due to engine and propeller noise. The elevated noise interferes with biological communication in the same frequencies. We used a monthly metric to assess noise interference. Ocean sound data was combined with ship tracking data. Nearby ship presence is tracked using data from the Automatic Identification System (AIS), an onboard transponder that most ships are required to use to transmit their location and identity. Higher median values indicate more interference with biological communication at the specified frequency.

How does the level of traffic change conditions?

Ship traffic can vary and this may influence the level of noise in the environment. Periods with different numbers of ships transiting nearby were compared to understand the difference: no ships, low number of ships ( less than 3), medium number of ships (3 to 5 ships ), and high number of ships (more than 5).
